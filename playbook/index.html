The premise

Belonging is a business — if you refuse to run it like one.

Before I opened a clubhouse, I joined four social clubs in Chicago and paid anywhere from $150 to $2,000 a month. Every one of them nickel-and-dimed me at the door and hoped I wouldn't show up.

"My final bills every month were $2,000 — and I wasn't really doing anything."The founding insight

So Three Cities Social was designed as the inversion: BYOB, everything included, no bar minimums, no bottle service, no celebrity in the corner booth. A 7,500-square-foot clubhouse open 7am to midnight, running about 90% unmanned on badge access, where the whole point is the person at the next table.

Research says a friendship takes roughly 70 hours to form. Apps can't compress that. Bars can't hold it. A clubhouse can — because a physical home base is the validation of "I'll see you again." Everything in this playbook serves that one mechanic.

Part one

The Ten Laws of the Clubhouse

Every law below was paid for with real money or real embarrassment. Each one comes with its receipt.

Law I

Charge to show up.

Free things get treated like free things. Attach even a small, refundable commitment and attendance transforms.

Receipt: Our free kickball team of 25 forfeited ~80% of its games. Our $50-deposit volleyball league — credit refunded for attending — never forfeited once.
Law II

Your price is your price, forever.

Grandfather every member at their joining rate, permanently. It converts every price increase into a loyalty reward for the people already in the room — and an urgency engine for the people who aren't.

Receipt: "The price you're paying per month is the price you're going to pay forever, no matter how much we grow." Members quote this line back to me.
Law III

Build the container. Let members make the club.

The founder's job is infrastructure, not entertainment. Recruit member curators, give them the space free, and let them keep 100% of their ticket revenue.

Receipt: Curator cohorts — a handful of members each hosting monthly — plus fitness partnerships got us to 16–20 events a month at near-zero programming cost.
Law IV

The depth of membership is the product.

Your most valuable inventory is who's already inside. Experts trade programming for membership; influencers trade reach for hosting rights. Pay in belonging, not cash.

Receipt: Our Residency program: four experts (career, nutrition, finance, AI) each run recurring events and office hours — compensated with a plus-one membership, not a fee.
Law V

Rent the room. Never sell the room.

Private-event rentals are the margin that keeps the lights on — but every buyout closes your club to the people who pay dues. Cap rentals and program an off-site member event during each one.

Receipt: "Private event rentals are what keep the lights on here." Capped at 1–2 a month anyway.
Law VI

No second strike.

Culture is enforced at the moment it's cheapest to lose revenue and hardest to do — when the club is small. One serious conduct violation is one too many.

Receipt: The first removal happened when we had roughly 20 members. That decision is why there's a club at 300.
Law VII

Don't take the money.

Venture capital and community businesses want different things. The moment growth outranks belonging, members can smell it.

Receipt: "VCs kill community-based organizations." I watched a heavily funded club change its pricing five times and go corporate. We funded expansion with a private family loan on nine-year terms instead.
Law VIII

All-inclusive beats à la carte.

Every surcharge is a small resentment. BYOB means no liquor license, no bar minimums, and no $19 cocktails standing between a member and a Tuesday visit.

Receipt: The clubs I quit all billed me à la carte. The club I built includes everything — and runs 90% unmanned on badge access, 7am to midnight.
Law IX

Cap the club.

There's a number where intimacy dies. Decide yours before you reach it, and let scarcity do your marketing.

Receipt: ~500 members per house is our ceiling — "the healthy number to keep the lights on" and still know names. Fun fact: Soho House reportedly still has its first 500 members.
Law X

Write it down before you need it.

The test for every process: if the founder disappeared tomorrow, does the club survive the month? Playbooks aren't bureaucracy — they're the only exit from being the product yourself.

Receipt: "If I die tomorrow, we'd be totally screwed" is the sentence that started the document you're reading.
I.
Phase one · 0 → 25

The Container

Everything before the lease. This phase is cheap, unglamorous, and where most would-be founders skip straight to the fun part — and die.

Run the club before the club exists

Three Cities ran as a free events group for almost two years before a single lease was signed. That's not caution — that's product development. You learn which formats repeat, who your organizers are, and whether people come back without a space doing the work for you. The waitlist you build here is the founding cohort you'll convert later.

The napkin test for a space

  • Walkable neighborhood — the club should be on the way home, not a destination trip.
  • Rent under $8,000/month for your first room. The lease is the one mistake you can't iterate out of.
  • BYOB-permissive zoning — no liquor license means lower costs, lower risk, and an all-inclusive member experience.
  • Residential + cultural density — you want members who can stumble in on a Tuesday.
Hard-won detailCheck your city's assembly/amusement licensing before you sign — we needed a Public Place of Amusement license, zoning approval, and owner fingerprinting in Chicago, and used a permit consultant to navigate it. Budget weeks, not days.

The founding cohort: give away year one

Our first 25 members paid nothing for the first year. Heresy? That free year bought the three things no ad budget can: a full room on opening night, two dozen evangelists, and twelve months of programming feedback. When the year ended, we converted them with a simple, honest offer — lock in $199/month before the public price went to $249 — and the grandfathering promise (Law II) did the rest.

One funnel. Literally one.

Our entire acquisition machine was: see us somewhere → visit the website → book a tour. Half of all early signups came through a free neighborhood walking tour — a zero-pressure front door that let people experience curation before buying membership. The numbers behind the funnel:

Nothing about this funnel is clever. That's the point — one repeatable motion, executed hundreds of times, beats five clever ones executed occasionally.

II.
Phase two · 25 → 300

The Engine

A club is a machine with three moving parts: programming that creates repetition, onboarding that creates belonging, and retention that notices before the member does.

Programming: the five-format calendar

Every event we run fits one of five formats — Move, Talk, Play, Serve, Social — and the calendar is built so each format has a rhythm: a weekly anchor (Friday happy hour), monthly signatures (a mystery-dinner series, a neighborhood-exploration series), seasonal marquees (boat outings, the vendor market, the annual trip), and member-led micro-groups underneath it all.

Why leagues outperform everythingA league is the same small group, every week, with a shared goal — which is precisely the friendship formula (remember: ~70 hours). Our season format: 6 teams, 6 events, points for performance and participation and team spirit. Add a commitment deposit (Law I) and you have the highest-retention programming that exists.

The curator flywheel

You cannot personally throw 30 events a month, and you shouldn't try. The flywheel: recruit a rotating cohort of member curators each quarter. Each hosts one or two events monthly. Members attend free; curators sell public tickets and keep 100% of the revenue. One of our curators sells out at $95 a ticket. The club gets a full calendar and a stream of ticket-buying prospects walking through the door; curators get a stage and an income; members get programming with a soul. Everyone is overpaid in the currency they care about.

Onboarding: the first 30 days decide the first year

Most churn is decided in month one and merely executed later. Our choreography for every new member:

  • Welcome inside 24 hours — a personal message, not an automation.
  • Four touchpoints: Day 1, Week 1, Month 1, Month 2 — each owned by a named person, not a "team."
  • Two recurring events + three hosted introductions + one small group in the first 30 days. We track it.
  • Ambassadors: every new-member cohort is paired with trained veteran members whose only job is making the room feel familiar.
"The first 100 joined a movement. The next 200 joined a club."Why onboarding has to get more deliberate as you grow

Early members self-organize — they're founders too, in spirit. Everyone after them needs architecture: assigned small groups of 8–12 on 90-day cycles, connectors working every event, a "first three events" rule so nobody's third visit is them standing alone by the drinks table.

Retention: friction where it helps, none where it hurts

  • Application-gated membership. We added an application and a personal acceptance note from the founder — not to be exclusive, but because "the friction filters out the people who were never serious, which is most of our churn problem."
  • Health scores by the member's own goals. "Healthy" isn't event attendance — it's whether they're getting what they said they came for. Lifecycle stages: New → Growing → Established → Retained, reviewed weekly.
  • Exit interviews on every cancellation. A 20-minute conversation, personally. Most leavers say yes or maybe to returning — treat every cancel as a future rejoin.
  • The win-back offer: original rate honored, fee waived. The rate-lock (Law II) also works in reverse: cancel and you can't rejoin at your old price. It's a churn brake that requires no discounting.
  • Kill pauses before they kill you. We let members pause memberships for a year — until people strung pauses together and revenue planning became fiction. Ending them hurt for a week and helped forever.

Rituals: the club's heartbeat

One prompt every Friday morning from the club's house account. Introductions on a different weekday, so members never feel pinged twice in a day. A monthly recap email that celebrates attendance numbers, welcomes new members by name and neighborhood, and teases what's next. The rule underneath all of it: one gentle nudge at a time — a club that shows up for you, never an app that buzzes.

III.
Phase three · Unit economics

The Money

Here are numbers club founders never publish. I'm publishing them because pretending this business is easy helps no one — and because the model genuinely works when you respect the math.

Revenue is a three-legged stool

62%Membership dues
29%Private events
9%Sponsors

Actual revenue mix, March 2026: ~$33.0K membership MRR · $15.4K private events · $5K sponsorship = $53.4K month

Dues are the foundation — recurring, predictable, and the thing everything else protects. Private rentals are the margin: a whole-club buyout runs $2,600 per four hours plus hourly overages, with deposits, a fines schedule, and a bartender requirement above 40 guests, all in a real contract. Sponsorship is the upside: our anchor brand deal pays $5K per quarter, and the trick is conversion — when a company asks about a $10K one-day rental, sell them a year-round partnership instead.

The line that changes how you think"Every new member at $199–249 is nearly pure margin, since fixed costs are already covered." Once the room is paid for, growth is profit. This is why capping the club (Law IX) is a strategy, not a sacrifice — and why every retention system in Phase II is worth more than any acquisition channel.

What I killed, and why

  • Paid ads as the main engine. Meta ads worked — roughly 15 members a month at $4–5K spend — but CAC hit ~$500/member. We rebuilt around referrals, partnerships, QR codes, and listing select events publicly. Today the funnel is ~94% direct and word-of-mouth. Rule: one-month CAC payback or it doesn't scale.
  • A $5K/month PR firm. Months in: one placement, which I'd sourced myself. The same $5K in ads bought 15 members. Fired via the contract clause. Demand a KPI tracker from week one.
  • A $1,600/month video vendor. Beautiful content, unmeasurable return. A phone and a member with taste got 90% of the value.
  • Lifetime memberships. Never sold one. Tempting cash today, poison for every future decision — including selling or expanding.
  • Membership pauses. See Phase II. A $20/month pause fee softened the landing, but the real fix was ending them.
IV.
Phase four · Expansion, honestly

The Second House

We opened a second clubhouse fifteen months after the first. Twenty-one months later, I chose not to renew its lease. Both decisions were right. This chapter is the one most consultants can't write.

The case for going

Roughly 30% of our members lived in Wicker Park. A sublessor had written off their lease as a sunk cost, which is exactly the counterparty you want: we negotiated $10K/month on an $18–19K ask — 45% below — on 8,375 square feet. The launch playbook was already proven: interest list → tour calendar → "founding member spots" scarcity → free-trial bridge with a joining credit. It worked again: founding members signed before the doors opened.

The case I didn't want to hear

The honest model said the sublease alone was roughly a negative-2% IRR — the bet only worked if the long-term renewal penciled. Buildout was budgeted at $125K and landed near $300K. Steady-state, the two-location P&L ran about $10K/month negative against a low-six-figure reserve. As I put it in a strategy session that winter: "Even if we lose $5–10K a month right now, that's not a good runway."

The bravest thing I did as a founder wasn't opening the second clubhouse. It was declining the $1.4 million renewal.

When the renewal came — five years, ~$23K/month escalating — the math said no, and this time I listened before signing. We consolidated to the flagship, kept the members, kept the programming, and kept the company. The second house wasn't a failure; it was tuition. Here's the checklist it bought:

The expansion gates (all four, not three)

  • Bank balance rising for months — not stable. Rising. Expansion eats reserves faster than any model shows; assume buildout runs 2x.
  • Retention proven, not assumed — if location one leaks members, location two leaks money.
  • Processes that pass the "if I die tomorrow" test — you cannot be in two clubhouses at once. If the club needs you daily, you have no business doubling it.
  • A lease your worst case can carry — we quantified the worst case at $90K before signing. Know your number, and only bet what the first house can absorb.
The reframeExpansion isn't the only way to grow. The same energy pointed at the flagship — deeper programming, sponsorships, private events, capped-membership pricing power — grows revenue without a second rent check. Density beats geography until density is exhausted.

Phase five · Founder sustainability

The Operating System

The #1 risk in this business — named in every plan we ever wrote — is over-reliance on the founder. Here's the system that turned me from the club's single point of failure into its architect.

The themed week

DayThemeThe non-negotiable
MondayGrowth & strategy45-minute team sync — four timed blocks, pre-filled, every item leaves with an owner and a deadline. "If it runs over 45 minutes, something is wrong."
TuesdayMembers & revenueReview MRR and cancellations; take one action. Primary tour block.
WednesdayProgramming & contentBuild ONE new event concept fully — "not ten halfway." Brief the content contractor; the founder briefs and approves, never executes.
ThursdayOps & presenceUpdate one SOP. Then an hour at the clubhouse with no agenda — presence is the product.
FridayReview & prep20-minute review, six questions, numbers flagged. No tours. "Week is done by noon."
WeekendThe clubStaff own event execution. The founder attends as founder — not as coordinator.

The financial rhythm

Two inputs a month: a Stripe export on the first Tuesday ("two minutes") and a one-line note of non-membership revenue. From those, a monthly one-page P&L, an MRR roster, and flags on anything drifting. AI drafts the analysis, event descriptions, and member comms; the founder edits and sends. Everything lives in one project so nothing is re-explained twice.

The stack, by stage

  • Phase I (0–25): a form, a calendar tool, a group chat, a booking link. Genuinely nothing else. Every dollar into the space and the events.
  • Phase II (25–300): membership platform with billing · door access hardware · room booking · e-signature · email · a CRM for tours · a work-management board for retention tracking.
  • The warning: platform migrations are churn events. Ours produced double-charges, password chaos, and "which app??" for weeks. Migrate with a formal plan, a hard cutoff date, and a billing bridge — or don't migrate.
  • Vendor rule: the quote is not the invoice. We were quoted $250/month in cleaning supplies and billed $1,100/month. Read line items monthly; get cancellation clauses in writing; assume every critical vendor (the boat captain, the internet install, the insurance) will fail at the worst moment — and have a second name for each.

The hiring design

First real hire: a community director — as a contractor first, to de-risk. Three rounds: a short screen, an in-person tour with homework (bring three event ideas), then a presentation to a member panel — advisory, but nothing tells you more than watching a candidate hold the room. First-quarter goals are volume goals (give ten tours), not conversion goals: you're testing the engine, not the salesperson.
